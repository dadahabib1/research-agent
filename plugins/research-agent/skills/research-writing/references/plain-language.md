# Plain-language checklist

A condensed checklist drawn from the US Federal Plain Language Guidelines (2011, maintained in the plainlanguage.gov archive), in our own words. The guidelines are a US Government work in the public domain; see `SOURCES.md`. Run it when editing a draft, after the research-writing skill's sentence-level rules.

## Audience

- Name the reader, what they already know, and what they must do after reading. Write to that one person, not to experts, lawyers or management unless they are the reader.
- Plain does not mean simplified. Match the reader's level of knowledge; do not write down to them.
- Tell the reader early why the document matters to them.
- If two audiences need different things, give each its own section.

## Organisation

- State the purpose and the bottom line first. Background, if needed, goes towards the end.
- Order sections by what the reader needs first, usually by the questions they will ask.
- Use many headings. Prefer, in order: questions the reader would ask, then statements, then topic labels. A bare noun ("Methodology") is the weakest heading.
- Open each paragraph with its topic sentence.
- Use lists for steps and for parallel items. Avoid lists within lists.
- Use transition words ("so", "however", "for example") to show how one idea leads to the next.
- Put the main clause before exceptions and conditions. Put a short condition first only if reading it later would mislead.

## Words

- Choose the word the reader already knows, and the short, concrete one over the long, abstract one.
- Use verbs, not hidden verbs: "we estimated the effect", not "we performed an estimation of the effect". Watch for endings such as -tion, -ment and -ance joined to verbs like make, give, have or conduct.
- Break up strings of more than three nouns by adding prepositions and articles.
- Use the same term for the same thing throughout. Synonyms for variety confuse readers.
- Keep abbreviations to two or three per document. Prefer a plain short name ("the survey"). Spell out each abbreviation on first use; skip the expansion only for ones every reader knows.
- Define as few terms as possible, and never give a common word an uncommon meaning.
- Write out "for example" and "that is"; many readers confuse e.g. and i.e.

## Concision

- Question each word and cut those the reader can do without: intensifiers ("very significant"), modifiers the context already implies, and phrases built on prepositions ("in regard to the matter of").
- Write short sentences, one idea each. Split long sentences with several conditions into separate sentences or a list.
- Keep paragraphs short and on one topic: as a guide, under 150 words and never over 250. An occasional one-sentence paragraph is fine.
- Keep sections short; a long section usually hides two topics.
- Keep subject, verb and object close together.

## Voice and tone

- Use the active voice, so it is clear who does what.
- Address the reader as "you" where the document tells them what to do.
- Use the present tense for facts and rules.
- Use "must" for requirements; avoid "shall".
- Use examples to explain complex ideas; one good example can replace a paragraph of explanation.
- Avoid slashes ("and/or"); say which you mean.

## Design

- Use tables for complex comparisons, and if-then tables for rules that depend on conditions.
- Highlight sparingly; emphasis on everything is emphasis on nothing.
- Minimise cross-references. If the reader needs a fact here, state it here.
- Use visuals where they explain faster than text.

## Test

- Test early and again after revising. Do not wait for a final draft.
- Paraphrase test: have a reader stop at set points and say in their own words what the text means and what they would do. Record their words; do not correct them. Rewrite wherever their account differs from what you meant.
- Ask open questions, not yes-or-no ones. Asking what other readers might find hard surfaces problems people will not admit to having themselves.
- For research deliverables, a fresh agent with no context can run the paraphrase test on the decisions section.
